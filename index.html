Part one

Research isn't just for
"future scientists"

Whatever you're aiming at, it pays off. Here's how.

~30 sec. Transition: "Before the networking part — why bother with research at all? Quick show of hands: who's thinking pre-med? Who's thinking research/PhD? Who has no idea? Good — there's a slide for each of you."

If you're an aspiring physician

Research is the differentiator

  • Med schools expect it. Research experience is one of the most common threads among successful applicants.
  • Medicine is research. Every drug, protocol, and guideline you'll ever use started as somebody's study. Understanding how evidence is made makes you a better clinician.
  • It opens clinical doors. Labs connect you to physicians, hospitals, shadowing, and mentors years before you'd otherwise meet them.
~1.5 min. If you have a stat or anecdote from your own labs about physicians you've worked with, use it here. Point: research isn't a checkbox — it's how you learn to read medicine critically.

If you're an aspiring researcher or PhD

Start before you're "ready"

  • Authorship compounds. Starting in high school or early college means publications, posters, and letters years ahead of the curve.
  • Mentors change trajectories. One PI who believes in you is worth more than any GPA point.
  • Try before you commit. A PhD is a 5+ year decision. Working in a real lab now tells you whether you actually love the day-to-day — before it costs you anything.
~1.5 min. Your preprints are the proof here — three submitted before most people write their first. Be honest about the grind too: rejection, revisions, slow cycles.

If you're neither — or not sure yet

Research is a critical-thinking gym

Read hard things

Dense papers stop being scary. That skill transfers to law, business, engineering — everything.

Ask real questions

You learn to turn "that's interesting" into a testable, answerable question.

Sit with ambiguity

Most experiments fail or half-work. You get comfortable not knowing — and finding out.

Defend your reasoning

Lab meetings teach you to make a claim, back it with evidence, and take criticism without flinching.

~1.5 min. This is the slide for the undecided kids — don't skip it. "Even if you never set foot in a lab again, one year of research rewires how you think."

Demystified

What research actually looks like

What people imagine

  • Geniuses in lab coats
  • Eureka moments daily
  • You need to know everything first

What it really is

  • Reading papers & asking questions
  • Cleaning data, running analyses, meetings
  • Slow, steady progress — learnable by anyone

It's not genius-gated. It's effort-gated.

~1.5 min. Describe your actual week in the lab — the unglamorous parts. This lowers the barrier: nobody in the room should think "that's not for people like me."

Part two

Opportunities move
through people

The best positions are never posted. Someone tells someone about you.

~30 sec. Transition into networking. "Every lab seat I've had, someone had to say yes to a person they barely knew. That's what networking actually is — becoming easy to say yes to."

Redefined

Networking ≠ schmoozing

It's curiosity

Asking people genuine questions about their work. People love talking about what they do — let them.

It's follow-through

Sending the thank-you note. Circling back in a month with what you did with their advice.

It's being easy to help

Specific asks, quick replies, doing what you said you'd do. Helpable people get helped.

You're not extracting favors.
You're starting relationships.

~2 min. Kill the "networking is fake/icky" objection head-on. Story: a relationship you kept warm that paid off much later. Mention that your collaborators at Brigham & Women's / Yale / Vanderbilt are relationships, not transactions.

The mindset

Worst case

Best case

Everything.

A lab seat. A mentor. A recommendation letter. A career path you didn't know existed.

  • Researchers want interested students. A thoughtful email from a motivated high schooler is rare — and memorable.
  • My labs trace back to unsolicited asks. Nobody came looking for me. I showed up in inboxes.
  • The asymmetry is absurd. Five minutes of courage, potentially years of payoff. Send the email.

The craft

Anatomy of a cold email

To: dr.rivera@university.edu

Subject: Question about your sleep & memory fMRI study — Waukee APEX student 1

Dear Dr. Rivera,

I'm Sarah Chen, a junior in Waukee APEX's Medical & BioScience Research program in Iowa. 2

I recently read your paper on hippocampal activity during sleep consolidation, and the finding that targeted memory reactivation strengthened recall stuck with me — I've been trying to design a small study on sleep and learning myself. 3

Would you be open to answering two short questions over email about how your lab designs imaging studies? I'd be grateful for even a brief reply. 4

I completely understand if you're too busy — thank you for reading either way. 5

Sarah Chen

  1. 1
    Specific subject line
    Names their actual work + who you are. Never "Opportunity inquiry."
  2. 2
    Who you are, one line
    Enough context to place you. No life story.
  3. 3
    Proof you did homework
    Reference something they made. This is what separates you from spam.
  4. 4
    One small, concrete ask
    Easy to say yes to. Not "can I work in your lab" on email one.
  5. 5
    Easy out + gratitude
    Respect their time. It reads as maturity.
~2.5 min. Walk the five parts in order. (Names in the example are fictional.) Emphasize #3 — the homework line — as the single highest-leverage sentence. Under 150 words total; busy people read short emails first.

Ground rules

Do / Don't

Do

  • Keep it under ~150 words
  • Make one specific, small ask
  • Reference their actual work
  • Use your school email & proofread
  • Follow up once after ~1 week

Don't

  • Send a wall of text or your résumé
  • Ask "are there any opportunities?"
  • Copy-paste the same email to 50 people
  • Attach files on the first email
  • Take silence personally — people are busy
~1.5 min. Quick-fire slide. On the follow-up point: one polite bump doubles your odds; five bumps gets you blocked. On silence: a non-reply is almost never about you.

Your turn · ~8 minutes

Draft your cold email

  1. Pick your field. The career you want — or are just curious about.
  2. Find one real person. A researcher, physician, or professional you'd genuinely want to learn from.
    Try: university lab pages · Google Scholar · hospital directories · LinkedIn
  3. Find their email. University directory, lab website, or the corresponding-author line on a paper.
  4. Draft it. Use the template on the next slide. Aim for under 150 words.
~1 min setup, then ~8 min work time. Everyone drafts a REAL email to a REAL person. Phones/laptops out. Circulate and help people find names. Offer: "If you send it this week, email me and tell me what happened."

The template

Subject: [Their specific work] — question from a Waukee APEX student

Dear Dr. [Last name],

1 I'm [name], a [year] in Waukee APEX's Medical & BioScience Research program.

2 I came across your [paper / project / talk] on [topic], and [the specific thing that interested you].

3 Would you be open to [one small ask: two questions by email / a 15-minute call / advice on X]?

4 I completely understand if you're busy — thank you either way.

[Your name]

Before you hit send

  • Under 150 words?
  • Their name spelled correctly?
  • Exactly one ask?
  • Something only they could have written about?
  • No attachments?
  • Reminder set to follow up in a week?
Work-time slide. Stay here while students draft. With ~2 min left, ask for a volunteer to read theirs aloud and workshop it kindly against the checklist.
