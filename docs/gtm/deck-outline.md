# Six-slide deck: copy and speaker notes

A short deck for a second conversation, a mentor session or a grant pitch. It is not for a first interview: there you ask questions.

Rules:

- No number from the simulated product. No screenshot of it.
- No prices. Prices go in a written proposal from the Padma domain, to people who asked.
- No Vanderbilt logo. No "patients". No earnings claims.
- Every slide carries the footer below.
- Six slides. If you want a seventh, cut one.

**Footer on every slide:** Padma is a student project in pilot stage. It has no enrolled participants yet. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Vanderbilt University or Vanderbilt University Medical Center.

---

## Slide 1: The problem

**Title:** Small studies stay small because finding people is slow, and going back to them is slower.

**On the slide:**

- Research teams that study everyday life need people who will log, wear a device and answer questions for two weeks.
- [How long finding them takes, in the words of the teams you interviewed.] We found recent published studies in this area built on 10 to 20 participants.
- When a team needs one more measurement, it usually starts recruiting again from nothing.

**Speaker notes:** Start with what you heard, not what you believe. "In [n] interviews this month, research teams told us [their words]." Use two short quotes from your notes, without names. If you have done fewer than five interviews, say so. You may say that you found recent published work in this area built on 10 to 20 participants; do not name the authors. Do not claim a market size.

---

## Slide 2: What Padma does today

**Title:** A registry of adults who want to be invited, and a service that reaches them.

**On the slide:**

- **A registry.** Adults sign up to be invited to research studies. It holds contact details only: no health information.
- **A referral service.** We send a research team's approved study notice to the registry members who opted in to that kind of study, and report what happened.
- **A working model of the software**, on simulated data, showing where this could go.

**Speaker notes:** Be exact about what is real. The registry is real: say the true number of sign-ups from the sheet on the day, and say they are leads, not a cohort. The service is real but new: say whether you have run it yet. The software is a model: all of its data is simulated. If someone asks to see it, offer a separate ten-minute walkthrough.

---

## Slide 3: How a pilot works

**Title:** Your protocol, your tools, your participants' consent. We bring people to the door.

**On the slide:**

1. You hold the ethics cover: your approved protocol names Padma as a recruitment source.
2. We send your approved notice to members who opted in, and recruit for your study through agreed channels.
3. People who are interested go to your screener and your consent form.
4. Data is collected in your tools, for 14 days per participant, using their own phone and wearable.
5. You pay participants directly. We invoice a service fee only.
6. You get a weekly report (notices sent, people who clicked through, people who started and finished) and, at the end, what the protocol sheet lists.

**Speaker notes:** The point of this slide is what Padma does not do: it does not screen, does not take consent, does not keep study data, does not hold participant money, does not buy devices. Say that the study data belongs to the buyer's study. Devices such as glucose monitors and blood-pressure cuffs are for a second pilot, supplied by the buyer. If asked about price, say it is in the written proposal.

## Slide 4: What makes it different: recontact

**Title:** The same people, asked again.

**On the slide:**

- Most recruitment ends when the study ends.
- Registry members agree to hear from us again. Some will agree, study by study, to be measured again.
- Every pilot proposal has one optional line: go back to the same participants for one more measurement.
- We are testing this openly: by asking research teams what going back was worth, by measuring whether members answer a month after joining, and by seeing whether buyers choose that line.

**Speaker notes:** This is a hypothesis, and you should call it one. Say what you know so far: "[x] of [y] buyers we interviewed had gone back to the same participants; they told us it cost [their words]." After the check-in test has run, give the two real numbers: "[x] of [y] members answered within 7 days." Until then, say the test is scheduled. Do not say members are "engaged".

## Slide 5: What we are asking for

**Title:** [One ask, written for this audience.]

**On the slide** (choose one version):

- **For a startup:** A design-partner pilot: 10 participants, one protocol, a weekly call. We send a one-page proposal this week.
- **For a lab:** 20 minutes with the person who runs your recruiting, and, once the ethics office has answered, one study to trial a referral campaign.
- **For a mentor or programme:** Two introductions to people who run studies with participants. An hour of legal advice. A place in the next grant round.
- **For a grant panel:** Funding for two first steps: forming the company, and independent ethics review of Padma's own protocol.

**Speaker notes:** One ask per audience. Say it, then stop. For a lab, do not mention a fee. For a grant panel, say what the money buys in order: the company first (so a buyer can pay), then ethics review (so the data can become Padma's to license). Do not promise revenue dates you cannot keep; the plan's honest dates are a startup scope by mid-February and university money not before March.
